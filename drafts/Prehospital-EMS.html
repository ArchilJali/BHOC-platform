Prehospital / EMS · Hemorrhage

The bleeding starts before the hospital. So does the oxygen-delivery crisis.

Prehospital transfusion is expanding because severe hemorrhage creates a narrow interval in which blood loss, perfusion, oxygen-carrying capacity and transport time interact. But the larger systems question goes beyond transfusion itself: how do we preserve the life-saving function of oxygen delivery before definitive hospital care? This is the transition from prehospital blood transfusion toward prehospital Precision Oxygenation.

01 / The system gap

More than 400 programs — and still more than 97% without onboard blood.

The most important number is not 400 by itself. It is the denominator. Prehospital blood programs are growing rapidly, but the Coalition reports that fewer than 3% of eligible ground EMS agencies currently carry blood products.

What happens during the transport interval when catastrophic hemorrhage occurs in the large majority of EMS systems that do not have blood onboard?

02 / Human cost

Nearly half may die before reaching the hospital.

The Prehospital Blood Transfusion Coalition states that nearly half of the more than 10,000 Americans who die annually from severe blood loss die before hospital arrival. That corresponds to a prehospital burden on the order of ~14 deaths per day.

This should not be written as “14 lives could be saved each day.” It is a measure of the size of the prehospital mortality window. The actual preventable fraction depends on injury severity, hemorrhage control, time, transport, transfusion strategy and the rest of the trauma system.

03 / Why oxygen delivery matters

Blood loss is visible. Tissue oxygen debt is the downstream problem.

Hemorrhage control remains primary. Blood products remain standard when indicated and available. But the physiological reason transfusion matters is not simply the hemoglobin number — it is restoration of circulating volume, oxygen-carrying capacity and tissue perfusion. And even when blood is given, adequate tissue oxygenation is not guaranteed if microvascular flow remains impaired, oxygen unloading is suboptimal, or oxygen does not effectively reach the ischemic tissue.

This creates a disciplined BHOC research question: could a validated Precision Oxygen Therapeutic serve as a bridge or complement when donor blood is unavailable, delayed or operationally difficult to deploy — and in selected settings where transfusion alone does not adequately restore tissue oxygen delivery?

05 / A larger trauma-system benchmark

Potentially preventable deaths show why the system matters.

The National Academies estimated that, with optimal trauma care, as many as 20% of US trauma deaths in 2014 — nearly 30,000 deaths in one year — might have been preventable.

08 / BHOC research relevance

From prehospital blood transfusion to prehospital Precision Oxygen Therapeutics — preserving the life-saving function of oxygen delivery.

Preserving the life-saving function of oxygen delivery before definitive hospital care.

Donor blood and whole blood remain central where available and indicated. The next step is not to replace transfusion, but to extend the same life-saving objective — preservation of oxygen delivery — into the interval and geography where blood is unavailable, delayed or operationally difficult to deploy.

Research framing: Could a validated, deployable oxygen-delivery therapeutic help preserve tissue oxygenation during the critical prehospital window until definitive hemorrhage control and blood-component therapy become available?
