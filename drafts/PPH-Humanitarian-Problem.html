PPH · Humanitarian problem

One maternal death about every three minutes in sub-Saharan Africa.

Postpartum haemorrhage is not only an obstetric emergency. In many settings it becomes a systems problem: rapid blood loss, delayed recognition, limited transfusion infrastructure, distance to definitive care and a shrinking window in which tissue oxygen delivery can be preserved.

01 / The humanitarian reality

The problem is not lack of knowledge alone. It is speed, blood availability, transfusion infrastructure, access and implementation.

Modern obstetric care has effective tools for prevention, diagnosis and treatment of postpartum haemorrhage. Yet outcomes remain sharply unequal. In many conflict-affected, rural and resource-limited settings, the limiting factor is not only clinical knowledge or recognition of PPH. It is whether compatible blood is actually available, whether a blood bank and cold chain exist, whether screening, storage and transfusion infrastructure are functioning, and whether trained teams, transport, laboratory support and definitive surgical care can be reached in time.

The question: how do we preserve oxygen delivery during the interval between catastrophic blood loss and definitive haemorrhage control?
Why oxygen delivery enters the discussion

Bleeding is visible. Tissue hypoxia is the downstream threat.

PPH management must stop the bleeding first. But severe blood loss can also reduce circulating oxygen-carrying capacity and perfusion. That makes oxygen delivery a physiological endpoint worth tracking alongside haemorrhage control, transfusion and resuscitation.

  • Haemorrhage control remains primary.
  • Transfusion and blood components remain standard when indicated and available.
  • Any future oxygen-delivery therapeutic would need product-specific evidence and should be evaluated as a complement, not a universal blood replacement.

05 / BHOC research relevance

The future question is operational as much as molecular.

If an oxygen-delivery therapeutic can be stored, deployed and administered under conditions where donor blood is delayed or unavailable, that could create a meaningful research hypothesis for PPH and humanitarian medicine. But the page should explicitly separate clinical need from proven product benefit.

Research framing: Could a validated oxygen-delivery therapeutic extend the physiologic bridge to definitive haemorrhage control in selected PPH settings?
