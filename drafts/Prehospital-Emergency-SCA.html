Prehospital Emergency · SCA · Hemorrhage

Before the hospital, minutes become physiology.

Sudden cardiac arrest and catastrophic bleeding are different emergencies with different standard treatments. But both expose the same fundamental constraint: if circulation or oxygen-carrying capacity collapses, tissue oxygen delivery can fail long before definitive hospital care begins.

Pathway A / Sudden cardiac arrest

SCA is primarily a circulation failure.

~350k/year
US out-of-hospital cardiac arrest

When the heart stops pumping, brain and organ perfusion collapse within seconds. Immediate CPR, early defibrillation when indicated and high-quality resuscitation remain the established priorities.

Important: this is not a transfusion problem in most cases. SCA belongs here because it is an extreme oxygen-delivery failure state, not because blood products are standard treatment for cardiac arrest.
Pathway B / Catastrophic hemorrhage

Bleeding is a volume and oxygen-carrying problem.

>10k/year
US severe blood-loss deaths

The Prehospital Blood Transfusion Coalition reports that nearly half of these patients die before reaching hospital. That implies a prehospital burden on the order of ~14 deaths per day — not a count of people guaranteed to be saved, but a measure of how narrow the treatment window can be.

Established priorities: rapid hemorrhage control, appropriate resuscitation, blood products where indicated and available, and transport to definitive care.
01 / The implementation gap

Blood before hospital is growing — but it is still rare.

The most important story is not simply that more EMS systems are carrying blood. It is the contrast between demonstrated operational demand and limited nationwide penetration.

400+ programs sounds large. But if that still represents less than 3% of eligible ground EMS agencies, what happens to critically bleeding patients in the other 97%?

02 / How many lives are theoretically in the system gap?

Use an upper-bound benchmark — and label it honestly.

The National Academies estimated that, with optimal trauma care, as many as 20% of US trauma deaths in 2014 — nearly 30,000 deaths in a year — might have been preventable. That historical benchmark is about ≈82 potentially preventable trauma deaths per day.

≈82/day

Potentially preventable trauma deaths with optimal care

This is a system-wide historical estimate, not a prediction of lives saved by prehospital blood, whole blood, BHOC or any single technology. For prehospital hemorrhage specifically, the PHBTC figure of nearly half of >10,000 severe-blood-loss deaths occurring before hospital gives the narrower context of roughly 14 prehospital deaths per day.

03 / Evidence is moving fast

The question is no longer simply “whole blood yes or no.”

Recent randomized trials show why this page should be an intelligence resource rather than advocacy. In 2026, large prehospital trials found that whole blood was not superior to blood-component strategies in their studied systems. That does not negate the value of prehospital transfusion; it refines the question toward patient selection, timing, product choice, storage, logistics and endpoints.

05 / BHOC research relevance

Do not ask “Can BHOC replace blood?”

The stronger question is: where does an oxygen-delivery gap remain after we account for CPR, hemorrhage control, blood, plasma, transport and definitive care?

For hemorrhage, a future validated oxygen carrier might be investigated as a deployable bridge or complement where blood is unavailable or delayed. For SCA, any relevance would require a different evidence pathway focused on perfusion, ischemia-reperfusion and tissue oxygenation. These should remain separate research hypotheses.
